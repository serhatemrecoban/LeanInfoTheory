LeanInfoTheory: information theory in Lean 4.

In your own Lean project, you can define the model from your paper and prove the results specific to it. When a proof uses a familiar theorem (e.g. the chain rule for mutual information), you can import it from LeanInfoTheory and apply it to the random variables in your model.

The four conceptual layers of a proof

The figure illustrates four conceptual layers in the formalization of an information theory paper. The first contains the paper’s main theorem. The second contains the definitions, intermediate lemmas and estimates specific to its proof. These draw on information-theoretic definitions and results in the third layer, which in turn use general mathematics from mathlib in the fourth.

When each project develops the third layer for itself, readers must interpret those foundations separately for each paper. LeanInfoTheory aims to provide a shared third layer for finite discrete information theory. Once its definitions and theorem statements have been reviewed, readers can focus on the paper-specific work and on whether the shared results apply to the paper’s model, distribution and hypotheses. Lean checks the full proof across all four layers.

Formalize one result from your paper

  1. Set up a project for your paper. Follow the Lake setup guide to add a specific LeanInfoTheory release to your project. Commit lean-toolchain and lake-manifest.json so other researchers can build your proof with the same versions.
  2. State the result in Lean. Choose one theorem from your paper. Define the model it uses and write down its assumptions and conclusion in Lean. Read the statement back in the paper’s notation. Do the random variables, their joint law and the claimed conclusion agree with the paper?
  3. Apply a library theorem to your model. Suppose your paper models a signal X, an observation Y and a processed observation Z, all finite-valued. To prove I(X; Z) ≤ I(X; Y), first use your model’s assumptions to show that X → Y → Z is a Markov chain. Given that hypothesis, the library’s data processing inequality gives the bound. The worked Lean example shows how to apply it.

Does the library theorem match your paper?

Read the library theorem in your paper’s notation. Do its quantities have the same meaning, and does your model satisfy its assumptions? Which details matter depends on the result you want to use.

  • Finite alphabets. In the data processing example above, X, Y and Z take values in finite alphabets. The underlying sample space can still be infinite.
  • Bits and nats. LeanInfoTheory measures entropy in nats. To compare a value in nats with a bound expressed in bits, divide the value in nats by ln 2. For example, a fair bit has entropy ln 2 in nats and 1 in bits.
  • Decoding on the support. A theorem may guarantee that a decoder recovers the output for every source outcome of positive probability. If your paper claims recovery for every outcome, you also need an argument for outcomes outside the support.
  • Conditioning. In H(Y | X), X is the variable you condition on. If your proof uses the law of Y given X = x, check that P(X = x) > 0. When that probability is zero, the joint law leaves the conditional distribution at x undetermined.
  • KL divergence. On a finite alphabet, D(P ∥ Q) is finite exactly when every outcome with positive probability under P also has positive probability under Q. Check this support condition before using a real-valued formula for the divergence.

An example from a case study: Perfect Functional Representations

The formalization of On Perfect Functional Representations used LeanInfoTheory v0.1.0 unchanged. One proof asks when a functional representation is perfect. Suppose X, Y and Z are finite-valued, X and Z are independent, and a decoder recovers Y from X and Z on the support of the source law. Under these assumptions,

I(Y; Z) = H(Y | X)  ⇔  X → Y → Z.

The equality on the left is the paper’s definition of perfectness. The downstream proof uses the decoder and independence to reduce that equality to I(X; Z | Y) = 0. It draws on the library’s mutual information chain rules and its Markov characterization to obtain the equivalence.
